Where Laboratory Automation Work Breaks: A Mixed-Methods Study of Public Practitioner Discussions

Short title: Laboratory Automation Bottlenecks
Article type: Original Research
Target collection: NexusXp: The Connected Lab, SLAS Technology

Laboratory automation depends on a heterogeneous stack of device interfaces, vendor software, physical-resource models, orchestration systems, execution records, recovery procedures, and validation practices. Public troubleshooting discussions document these layers in operational detail, although discussion counts lack the denominators required for prevalence or reliability estimates. This study develops a mixed-methods framework for extracting defensible technical findings from a specialist public forum. The pilot contains 55 purposively selected discussions and 45 analytical episodes from a deliberately difficult 14-thread subset. Ten bottlenecks are organized across ecosystem knowledge, interfaces and representations, runtime coordination, evaluation, and AI-specific context. Quantitative measures use bounded units such as device-interface pairs, deployment manifests, physical-resource definitions, execution scenarios, scheduling fields, and test claims. Mean pilot scores were 63.9% for integration accessibility, 54.2% for deployment-manifest completeness, 67.0% for physical-definition completeness, and 52.5% for observability coverage. Two of six bounded test claims fully aligned the test object, environment, acceptance rule, observed evidence, and claim scope. The strongest descriptive relationship connected observability and recovery (phi = 0.452, lift = 2.353). The incident narratives independently support a mechanism in which incomplete command-state-material evidence constrains recovery after irreversible physical execution. The study contributes a boundary-centered taxonomy, executable metric definitions, a publication claim ledger, a minimum reproducible troubleshooting template, and a governed resolved-knowledge schema. These outputs position public technical communities as sources of operational measurement design while preserving strict limits on prevalence, causal, and vendor-comparison claims.

Keywords: laboratory automation; lab robotics; technical communities; mixed methods; reproducibility; self-driving laboratories
